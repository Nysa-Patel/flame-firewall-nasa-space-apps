The research behind Phenox

Phenox reads NASA’s own test records. This page lists the experiments those tests came from and what published research says about the same questions, so you can check our readings against it. Every link goes to the paper or to NASA’s report.

Where the tests come from

Most of the tests with a burn rate in our table come from the Flame Extinguishment Experiment (FLEX), which burned small fuel droplets on the International Space Station. The experiment was designed to find how little oxygen a flame needs, and how much inert gas it takes to put one out.

  • NASA reportMethanol Droplet Combustion in Oxygen-Inert Environments in Microgravity

    Nayagam, Dietrich, Hicks and Williams · 2013 · NASA Technical Reports Server

    Droplets of alkanes and alcohols were burned in still microgravity air with different amounts of oxygen and inert gas, to find each fuel’s limiting oxygen index. For methanol, the reported limit is around 12% to 13% oxygen by volume.

    How Phenox uses it: it is the best published check on our own methanol rows. See the comparison above.

    Read it on NASA’s report server (opens in a new tab)

  • Journal paperCool-flame extinction during n-alkane droplet combustion in microgravity

    Nayagam, Dietrich, Hicks and Williams · 2015 · Combustion and Flame

    Studies how n-alkane droplets, the family heptane belongs to, go out in microgravity.

    How Phenox uses it: context for the heptane rows, which are more mixed than the methanol rows.

    Open the paper (DOI) (opens in a new tab)

  • Journal paperMethanol droplet extinction in carbon-dioxide-enriched environments in microgravity

    Hicks, Nayagam and Williams · 2010 · Combustion and Flame

    Looks at methanol droplets going out when carbon dioxide is added to the air, in microgravity.

    How Phenox uses it: shows that the extinction rows in our table are an established way to study flammability limits.

    Open the paper (DOI) (opens in a new tab)

  • Journal paperIsolated n-heptane droplet combustion in microgravity: “Cool Flames” – Two-stage combustion

    Farouk and Dryer · 2014 · Combustion and Flame

    A study of n-heptane droplets in microgravity that examines “cool flames” and two-stage burning.

    How Phenox uses it: one reason heptane results near the limit can be mixed.

    Open the paper (DOI) (opens in a new tab)

Why oxygen and pressure move the result

Spacecraft cabins do not have to match Earth air, so the oxygen level and the pressure are the two settings that matter most. Phenox maps tests by exactly those two numbers.

  • Conference paperMicrogravity Flame Spread over Non-Charring Materials in Exploration Atmospheres: Pressure, Oxygen, and Velocity Effects on Concurrent Flame Spread

    Olson and Ruff · 2009 · SAE Technical Paper 2009-01-2489

    Tested a thin plastic fuel in microgravity across 21% to 85% oxygen and 5 to 16 psia, in air flows like a spacecraft’s ventilation. Flame spread depended on flow, oxygen and pressure. For this fuel the dependence on oxygen was weaker (a square-root relationship) than for a charring fuel tested earlier.

    How Phenox uses it: it supports mapping tests by both oxygen and pressure, and shows different materials respond differently, which is why Phenox reads one fuel at a time.

    Open the paper (DOI) (opens in a new tab)

  • Conference paperFire Safety in the Low-Gravity Spacecraft Environment

    Friedman · 1999 · SAE Technical Paper 1999-01-1937

    Reports that material flammability and fire spread in microgravity are significantly affected by air flow, oxygen concentration and the mix of other gases, and that this can be used to correct standard material tests.

    How Phenox uses it: the reason a reading is tied to a stated oxygen level and pressure, never to a material alone.

    Open the paper (DOI) (opens in a new tab)

  • NASA reportSolid Fuel Ignition and Extinction (SoFIE) Project on ISS

    Ferkul, Brown, Mroczka, Bhattacharjee, Fernandez-Pello, Miller, Olson and T’ien · 2019 · NASA Technical Reports Server

    A newer ISS facility that tests solid spacecraft materials at oxygen levels and pressures planned for NASA’s exploration cabins, partly to check whether the usual 1-g flammability tests predict low-gravity fires.

    How Phenox uses it: shows where new data on solid materials will come from. Phenox accepts community uploads so that data can be added.

    Read it on NASA’s report server (opens in a new tab)

Fire at spacecraft scale

Small droplet tests are not the whole story. NASA also burned larger samples inside an uncrewed spacecraft, to see how a real fire grows and how it affects the vehicle.

  • NASA reportSaffire: A Novel Approach to Study of Spacecraft Fire Safety Using Un-Manned Spacecraft

    Urban, Ferkul, Olson, Ruff and Easton · 2017 · NASA Technical Reports Server

    The first test of spacecraft fires at a scale relevant to real spacecraft, run aboard a Cygnus cargo vehicle after it left the station. The flame reached a steady size, and the size of the enclosure around the sample affected flame spread more than expected.

    How Phenox uses it: a reminder that small-scale readings do not scale directly to a real cabin, which is why every Phenox reading is a starting point and not a clearance.

    Read it on NASA’s report server (opens in a new tab)

  • NASA reportFire Safety Implications of Preliminary Results from Saffire IV and V Experiments on Large Scale Spacecraft Fires

    Urban, Ruff, Ferkul, Easton, Owens and Olson · 2021 · NASA Technical Reports Server

    Follow-up experiments that burned plastic and cotton-fabric samples inside the Cygnus spacecraft and looked at the safety implications, including the gases produced.

    Read it on NASA’s report server (opens in a new tab)

Why flames look different in space

On Earth, hot gas rises and pulls fresh air in, so a flame is a tall teardrop. With no gravity there is no rising, so a flame can be a round ball. The fire page on this site uses NASA’s own photograph of a candle burning in Earth gravity next to the same candle in microgravity (NASA Glenn, image GRC-1998-C-00486) to show this. Moon and Mars gravity have no photograph, so that page blends the two and says so.

  • Journal paperCan cool flames support candle flame burning in microgravity?

    Dietrich and T’ien · 2022 · Combustion and Flame

    A recent paper that asks this question about candle flames in microgravity.

What this page does not claim

These papers give the reading context. They are not inputs to it: every Phenox verdict is computed only from the NASA test rows, by the rule on the method page. A reading is a summary of how past tests ended. It is not a NASA flammability certification, and “goes out” never means safe to fly. For material selection, NASA’s own standard is NASA-STD-6001 (opens in a new tab).

All papers and reports are linked from their publisher or from NASA’s Technical Reports Server. Summaries here are our own short paraphrases of the published abstracts or titles; open the source for the full text and for anything we have not quoted. The candle photograph is NASA Glenn Research Center, image GRC-1998-C-00486. Phenox is a student project and has not been reviewed by NASA.
