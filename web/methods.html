How a reading is made

Every reading on Phenox comes from NASA tests that were lit on purpose, so the record tells us how each flame ended, not whether a material can burn. This page follows one question from the raw files to the word on the card, with every rule and formula the code uses.

1

From NASA's files to rows

NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics repository publishes each combustion investigation as spreadsheets, one row per test, with column names that differ from one investigation to the next. Phenox reads them all, finds the columns that hold oxygen, pressure, fuel, burn rate and outcome, and stores every test in one table in Tiger Data, a Postgres database. The original row is kept beside the cleaned values.

Oxygen becomes a fraction from 0 to 1 and pressure becomes atmospheres. Many investigations recorded oxygen or pressure once for the whole run rather than per test, so only some rows end up with both.

How each flame ended

Every test's outcome is read from the words NASA wrote down and put in one of four classes. Only the first two say how the flame ended, so only they are conclusive. A droplet that broke apart ended the test for a different reason, and a blank tells us nothing.

3

Buckets: grouping tests that ran at nearly the same conditions

Tests rarely repeat an exact oxygen level and pressure, so the database groups them into buckets 5 percentage points of oxygen wide and 0.1 atm of pressure tall, up to 2 atm. It is Postgres's width_bucket:

Oxygen bucketbO₂ = x0.05 + 1
Pressure bucketbp = p0.1 + 1

x is the oxygen fraction (0.21 for 21%), p the pressure in atm, and ⌊ ⌋ rounds down. Bucket b covers oxygen from (b − 1) × 5% up to b × 5%. Each bucket's centre is (b − ½) times its width.

4

Finding the nearest tests

For your atmosphere and a material, the worker looks at every bucket where that material was tested and picks the one whose centre is closest. Distance is counted in bucket widths, so 5 points of oxygen and 0.1 atm weigh the same:

d = ( x − cx0.05 )² + ( p − cp0.1 )²

cx and cp are the bucket's centre. Ties go to the bucket with more tests.

The worker then says how the atmosphere relates to what was tested:

RelationMeaning
Exact bucketYour atmosphere falls inside the nearest bucket.
InterpolationNot in a tested bucket, but inside the lowest-to-highest oxygen and pressure ever tested for this material.
ExtrapolationOutside that range: beyond anything tested.
5

The share that went out

Inside the nearest bucket, count the conclusive tests. With k flames that went out and m that kept burning:

Conclusive testsn = k + m
Share that went outŝ = kn

Broken-apart droplets and unrecorded results are not in n. They are still shown on the card, so you can see how much was left out.

6

From share to reading

The share is split into thirds. The cut points are Phenox's choice, documented here, not a NASA standard.

Goes outŝ ≥ 23
Mixed13 ≤ ŝ < 23
Keeps burningŝ < 13
No datad > 2, or n = 0, or nothing tested

Two rules then lower Goes out to Mixed, because a good record nearby says little about conditions nobody tried:

The relation is Extrapolationyour atmosphere is outside every oxygen and pressure tested for the material
x > highest oxygen testedyour atmosphere is richer in oxygen than any test of it
Goes out describes a flame that was already lit and then went out. It is never a clearance to fly a material. NASA qualifies materials with its own standard, NASA-STD-6001.
7

How sure: the 95% Wilson interval

Two out of two is 100%, and so is fifty out of fifty, but they do not deserve the same trust. Beside every share Phenox shows a range that the true share would fall inside 95 times in 100 if the tests were run again. It uses the Wilson score interval:

Centrec = ŝ + z²2n1 + z²n
Half widthh = z1 + z²n ŝ(1 − ŝ)n + z²4n²
Range[ c − h, c + h ], with z = 1.96

The simpler textbook range, ŝ ± z√(ŝ(1 − ŝ)/n), collapses to zero width at 0% or 100% and can spill below 0 or above 100% with few tests. Wilson stays inside 0 to 100% and stays honest at the ends.

Down to the depress floor

A cabin can lose pressure, so each material is read at four pressures from the nominal one down to the floor you set. The form takes pounds per square inch; the worker is asked in atmospheres.

Sweeppi = pnom − (pnom − pfloor) · i3,  i = 0, 1, 2, 3
Unitsatm = psia14.696,   x = oxygen %100

Materials are ranked by their reading at nominal pressure, then by their worst reading down the sweep, then by how many conclusive tests back them.

What this cannot tell you: every test here was in microgravity, and NASA's repository has no Moon or Mars gravity tests yet. Material names are matched from the start of a word in NASA's fuel names: pmma finds “2 cm pmma sphere” and dodecane finds “iso-dodecane”, but ethanol does not find “Methanol”. The card always shows the fuel name it matched.
Data: NASA Physical Sciences Informatics, loaded into Tiger Data. The rule on this page is the assess() function in the ranking page; if one changes, the other must change with it. Phenox's reading of historical test outcomes, not a NASA flammability certification.
